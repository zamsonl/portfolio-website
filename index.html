Both chairs: running the attack and the detection

I ran the red side and the blue side of the same exercise. A command-and-control listener reached through a tunnelling service, implants beaconing on a fixed interval, then caught from the SIEM alone. Being the attacker taught me more about detection than any rule template did.

Kali PurpleC2 & beaconing MITRE ATT&CKDetection engineering
exercise — red path vs. what the sensor saw
What it involved 5
  • RedA C2 listener on Kali Purple, exposed through a tunnel, with implants polling on a fixed interval.
  • BlueWatched the same activity land in Wazuh, then wrote the detection rules for that signature.
  • Proved from dataThe tunnel launders the destination, so the firewall, the tunnel edge and the C2 side all point at shared or NAT’d addresses. Only the DNS-resolution rules identified the actual internal victims.
  • The real findingAn egress path the sensor could not see at all. A rule cannot help on traffic you never collect — that gap mattered more than the catch.
  • Tested honestlyA plain implant to a clean host on 443 was missed. The tunnel was itself the signature; a bare beacon has none, so detection falls back to behaviour.

06 — Background

About

I am a final-year Cybersecurity student at Asia Pacific University. For the past year I ran security operations for Palmgold Management, a licensed gaming operator in Malaysia — the steepest learning curve I have had so far.

The habit I picked up from doing it alone is distrust of surface signals. A rule that loads is not a rule that works. A clean service start does not mean your rules loaded. A bulk request that reports success does not mean the documents were indexed. I have been caught by each of those, so now I check the artefact rather than the message about the artefact — and I have found more real problems that way than from any alert.

Running both sides of a purple-team exercise changed how I write detections. Being the attacker for a week taught me more about what a rule needs to catch than any template did, and the most useful thing to come out of it was not the catch — it was discovering a segment whose egress the sensor could not see at all.

Next I want to take the SOC from detection into response: automated blocking and host isolation through firewall and switch APIs, and interval-regularity analysis for beaconing that a rule engine cannot express. I am also looking for a team where I can learn from people who have run this at a larger scale.
